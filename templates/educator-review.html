How the mapping was built

1 · Align

The first North American pronunciation for each lowercase alphabetic CMUdict headword was aligned to its spelling with m2m-aligner.

2 · Audit

Patterns found in at least 100 distinct aligned headwords became review candidates; frequency alone never adds a teaching pattern.

3 · Review

Reusable correspondences were reviewed, and each shipped pattern received 20 familiar examples. Ambiguous, rare, overlapping, or name-heavy candidates were excluded.

4 · Bundle

A fixed local lookup connects CMUdict headwords only to reviewed patterns and character positions. Conservative additive passes recover reviewed patterns at alignment-chunk edges, across five verified silent-letter endings, and in pronunciation-matched suffixed forms.

Each word is capped at 10 points. The ten strongest words count, so a pattern reaches mastery at 100.

Additive recovery audit: 20,094 reviewed consonants were recovered at alignment-chunk edges; 2,614 patterns were unlocked by silent e in final -ed; 119 patterns were recovered across verified final mb, mn, gn, lk, and bt families; and 1,850 unchanged mappings were inherited by pronunciation-matched suffixed forms. Examples include rowed, lamb, sign, doubt, makes, and safely. No prior association or headword was removed. The exact dictionary, canonical alignment, and complete ledger are retained as development data and are not packaged in the extension.

Limits: statistical boundaries are not linguistic authority. CMUdict includes names, compounds, and dialect-sensitive pronunciations. Broad IPA is a compact educator label, not a pronunciation lesson. A mapped word can still contain sounds outside the reviewed bank.

Mastery mappings versus Guided annotations: a mastery mapping awards progress for a reviewed sound-to-spelling pattern. A Guided annotation can explain a spelling feature without treating a soundless letter as a mastery category. In talk, for example, final k → /k/ earns mastery credit and the vowel is left unscored, while Guided explains the complete alk family: write a, then a quiet l, before k. This teaches the useful spelling context shared by talk, walk, and chalk without claiming that a always says /ɔ/. Other bounded dialect-sensitive families use the same non-scoring approach.

Development references: CMUdict, m2m-aligner, and CMUSphinx letter-to-phoneme notes.
